# Mastery Review

Measure what the learner can retrieve and use *without support*, then aim review at the gaps
that actually appeared. Familiarity, fluent rereading, and confidence are not mastery, and all
three feel exactly like it from the inside.

## Choose the evidence

Match the assessment to the goal, and sample more than one kind — a learner can be strong at
recall and unable to apply anything.

- **Recall** — state a definition, invariant, mechanism, or procedure from memory.
- **Explanation** — justify why it works; distinguish it from the nearest neighbouring idea.
- **Prediction** — trace code, a derivation, or a system's behavior before seeing the result.
- **Application** — solve a representative problem with minimal cues.
- **Transfer** — use the idea in a context it was not taught in, or compare two designs.
- **Debugging** — find and explain a planted misconception or a plausible failure.

Open with a short diagnostic, not a long generic quiz. Ask one question at a time in an
interactive session, unless the user asked for a worksheet or a full practice set. Skip trivia
unless exact recall genuinely matters for the work.

## Evaluate and remediate

After each answer, record the demonstrated strength and the precise gap. Grade as correct,
partially correct, incorrect, or *not enough evidence to tell* — that fourth verdict is the one
usually missing, and collapsing it into "correct" is how gaps survive review.

Let stated confidence steer what you probe next, never whether an answer counts.

For a gap, choose the smallest effective intervention: a cue, a contrasting case, a
counterexample, a prerequisite repair, or brief reteaching. Then reassess with a *different*
question. Repeating the same question back and getting the answer right measures short-term
memory, not learning.

## Review plan

When a plan is asked for, prioritize the observed weaknesses and the prerequisites that unlock
the most downstream material. State outcomes as observable performances, not topics covered.
Prefer short retrieval sessions distributed over time to one long rereading block, and mix
practice across topics once the individual skills are stable — blocked practice inflates
apparent mastery. Increase the delay and the difficulty as recall improves, and set a criterion
for advancing, revisiting, or retiring each topic.

Use calendar dates only when the user gave a deadline; otherwise describe intervals loosely.

Finish with a mastery map — secure, developing, gaps — and the single next practice task. Do
not call anything durable on the strength of one good answer.
